Question: How does replacing learned future predictions with simulator-derived embeddings change candidate evaluation, and how well does the latent goal cost align with task performance?

Protocol:
We run experiments for the Wall task in jepa-wms. We have a starting point and a goal, and a wall in between. The task is to find a path that reaches the goal from the starting point. 

Success Criteria:
Success means entering distance < 4.5 from the goal.
Any success outranks any failure.
Among successes, fewer actions before first success wins.
Among failures, smaller terminal doorway-aware remaining distance wins.

The original planning mechanism (setting A) is starting from a point and starting from mean 0 and sd 1, we use cross entropy method (CEM) to iteratively find the next steps. The idea is for 10 iterations, in each iteration we: 1. Generate 64 candidate action sequences. 2. Predict the embedding of the future "World" after the candidate moves have been applied and calculate the cost (remaining latent distance between the predicted endpoint and the goal embedding) for each. 3. Pick the top 10 candidate moves and use the mean and sd of those 10 "elites" as the new mean and sd.

After 10 iterations, We have 650 occurrences per episode: 64 evaluated sequences plus the updated mean for each of 10 rounds. After removing repeated sequences, we obtain 641 unique routes per episode. We repeat the experiment 10 episodes and got 6410 total routes. All were generated by A’s CEM; B and C rescore that shared pool.

For setting B, instead of using a World model to predict the future world embedding, we first replay each complete candidate sequence from the saved simulator state and encode its actual full-horizon endpoint. We then apply the same latent goal cost as A. Task outcomes stop at first success; any continuation afterward is used only for the full-horizon diagnostics. In this case, we can omit any inaccuracy caused by the world model not predicting an accurate future state.

For setting C, in addition to setting B, we directly use the real world next state calculated in B to calculate a real cost of the future state instead of converting it back to an embedding to calculate a latent cost. In this case, we have vision to everything and the move it chooses is the best route under our defined C criterion over the candidate pool. This serves as a reference group.

Results:

C is our reference, so We want to compare 

1. A's chosen final mean vs. A's minimum cost route under C's vision.
A's chosen final mean is better 1 time, ties 4 times, and is worse 5 times. However, they have the same number of successful trials (5/10). This shows that switching from the final mean to minimum A does not change success under those 10 trials, although it improves C outcomes in five and worsens one. 

2. A's minimum cost route vs. B's minimum cost route. 
A's minimums cost route has 5 success while B's minimum cost route has 7 successes. However A wins more comparisons by generally finding success trials with less steps and getting better results when both settings fail. This shows that knowing the true next state can produce more success but might not lead to optimal paths under latent cost.

3. B's minimum cost route vs. C's best route
Noticeably, B succeeds in all trials that a successful path exists in these captured pools. By knowing the true cost, one can find more direct routes in a candidate pool but even if the true cost is missing, the surrogate cost also can find successful (maybe not optimal) paths.

Limitation:
The setting has some limitations.
1. We only run 10 trials so the number of trials is small.
2. We only ran under the Wall environment for one model checkpoint and one configuration.
3. All candidates were generated by A’s CEM. B and C only rescore those candidates; we did not test candidate generation driven by B or C.
4. B scores the full-horizon endpoint, whereas C rewards first-hit success. Their disagreement therefore includes differences in temporal objectives.

Conclusion:
In this ten-trial Wall pilot, selecting with simulator-based latent costs increased success from five to seven trials. However, it did not consistently improve the full task ordering, and it matched the best available C outcome in only one trial. Both predicted dynamics and latent-objective alignment therefore warrant further investigation. Because B scores the full-horizon endpoint while C rewards first-hit success, their disagreement cannot be attributed solely to representation quality.
