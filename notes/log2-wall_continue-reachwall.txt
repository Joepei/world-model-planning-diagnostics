So for the follow-up experiments I have extended the naive Wall task to a more complicated task called Reach-Wall. The goal is to control a robotic arm to move close to a target (within 5cm of the target). It is a multi-step planning problem with 100 action budgets. Planning proposes a 30-action plan (or until action 100, whichever ends earlier) but execution executes 15 steps only (or until action 100).

Question 1: Same as before: How does replacing learned future predictions with simulator-derived embeddings change candidate evaluation, and how well does the latent goal cost align with task performance? 

Here C ranks full-horizon terminal TCP-to-goal distance; terminal success means distance ≤5 cm.

Procedure: For the original task, we have kept the same setting as Wall, where we solely computed the candidates under A's setting - World model for next state prediction, latent cost computation, etc. Then we apply B and C settings to rescore the same candidate sequences. Because this is a multi-step planning problem, A's chosen candidates for later steps are based on **its** chosen final elites. We cannot directly treat the setting as a multi-step problem for B and C as during each step, B and C will come up with different elites and theoretically sample different sets of follow-up steps. So to conduct fair comparison, I evaluated each saved decision state separately and just compared each step's final mean with B and C. This has created 336 decision pools in total (48 original episodes, each has 7 planning stages). 

Results: The results was pretty similar to the Wall results and expected: within the shared-pool experiment, B performs better on average, resulting 35/336 successes while A only has 14 in its deployed final means. B also has a lower mean terminal regret on average. The reference group C has 243/336 successes. 

So this supports the point that having access to the oracle agent improves conditional selection, but still misses many successful options already present in the pool.

=====

Question 2: Since question 1 constrain us to compare single step planning but this is a multi-step planning problem, what would happen if we actually allow each planner to carry out the full process? Starting from the same initial states and first sampled populations, how much can B and C improve upon A?

Procedure: Similar to the previous setting in which A uses a world model planner and a latent cost, B uses the oracle agent planner and a latent cost, and C uses the oracle agent planner and a real cost - success first, then earlier first-hit time, then terminal distance for failures. The difference is that instead of simply have B and C rescore and select from A's saved candidate sequences, we have B and C share just the initial state and first sampled population as A, but continue on future steps to carry out their own chosen moves and recalculate the new candidates from their new states. 

Results: The results were actually quite surprising in this case. B in this case is actually had lower observed episode success than A. Out of the 48 trials, C succeeded 48/48 times, and A succeeded 6/48 times. Surprisingly, B only succeeded 0/48 times. Although B has a lower mean distance at task termination (6.20 cm vs. A's 6.81 cm). Upon reviewing the detailed results, I found that B actually achieves more consistent proximity while A has a broader outcome distribution with wider tails. This wider tail allows A to cross more success threshold while having worse average distance at the same time. 

The next thing I tried to understand was to inspect the CEM planning failure mode, especially for B. Since the planning usually plans for 30 actions, but only 15 are carried out, there are two possibilities: 1. The proposed full plans often succeeded, but replanning repeatedly discards the useful suffix. 2. The full plans rarely succeed at all, even receiving low latent cost. To figure out which is true. I continuously replayed (and extended) selected plans from 15 actions to all 30 actions in each planning stage, and found that for B, in 43/48 episodes there is at least one successful route if full actions were carried out. Upon deep diving , I then asked whether the next search contained any successful executable prefixes, and whether the planner selected them. I found that at the corresponding successor states, the previous remainder remained feasible. Newly sampled candidates contained a successful 15-action prefix in 44 of 47 pools, yet none of those pools had a successful prefix among its final elites or returned mean. Thus, in these audited states, successful executable options were often available but were not retained by the endpoint latent objective. At this point, we can safely conclude that under the terminal latent objective, later CEM rounds improved terminal latent cost while successful executable prefixes became rarer.

There are still some limitations of the current experiment. E.g. I have observed that for the "30 planning 15 carry out" stage of the planning, B actually constantly out-performs A in remaining distance, however for a couple of the observed episodes, in the last 10 steps, which is "10 planning 10 carry out" stage, A demonstrates a sudden burst of improvement and can sometimes out-perform B. That behavior remains unstudied.
